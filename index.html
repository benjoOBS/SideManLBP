What's the room like?

What's this space most like?
(A classroom or office is "small," a church sanctuary or function hall is "mid-size," a gym or warehouse is "big open." This roughly sets what engineers call the room's reverb time — how long sound rings after it stops.)
What are the walls and floor mostly made of?
(Hard surfaces bounce sound around — engineers call that a "live" room. Soft surfaces soak it up — that's a "dead" or "treated" room.)
How high is the ceiling?
(More headroom generally means sound has more room to bounce before it reaches your ears.)
Will the room be full of people, or mostly empty?
(People soak up sound like soft furniture does. A packed room almost always sounds different — usually tighter — than an empty one at soundcheck.)
What are you mixing for?
(A tight, dry sound is often exactly right for a recording, but can just mean "that's the room" at a live show with natural reverberation. Livestream is its own case — your stream audience hears the room through a phone speaker or earbuds, where that same ambience often just reads as muddy, even in the same room as a live show.)
